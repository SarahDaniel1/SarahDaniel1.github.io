Working Papers

"Title"

Abstract

What drives individuals in cities to become politically involved in their neighborhoods? Existing research has primarily emphasized how the objective social and physical environ- ments in which individuals live shape political behavior. I argue that individuals do not respond directly to these environments as they objectively exist, but first to how they per- ceive and experience them. In particular, I argue that perceived neighborhood size plays an important role in shaping neighborhood-based civic engagement. I examine this possibility using evidence gathered through fieldwork in Nairobi, Kenya, including qualitative inter- views, mental maps, and a household survey experiment in which residents digitally draw their neighborhood boundaries and interact with these drawings throughout the experiment. I find that respondents who perceive their neighborhoods as smaller are more likely to engage in civic activities that improve neighborhood welfare and to exhibit more reciprocal behavior than those who perceive their neighborhoods as larger. Observational mediation analysis show that this relationship operates through increased perceptions of visibility among neighbors and stronger feelings of responsibility. Importantly, this research offers additional insights into how individuals in rapidly urbanizing cities across the Global South create alternative systems of support in response to limited service provision.

"Protest or Partnership? How Nigerian Women’s Organizations Navigate Repression"
(with Mango Jane Angar, Leonardo R. Arriola, Matthew K. Gichohi, and Liv Tønnessen)

Abstract

Despite Nigeria’s formal protections for civic activism, women’s rights organizations rarely engage in public protest. This article examines why protest remains an uncommon tactic among these groups even in moments of democratic openness and pressing gender policy debates. Drawing on interviews with women’s rights advocates and civil society leaders, we show that protest avoidance reflects a strategic adaptation to Nigeria’s hybrid political context. Organizations confront overlapping material constraints, reputational risks, and gendered norms that render confrontation costly and often counterproductive. In response, they adopt a repertoire of quiet advocacy by framing their work in technical terms, cultivating relationships with state actors, and engaging behind the scenes to influence policy. Rather than viewing the limited visibility of women’s movements as evidence of disengagement, we argue that tactical moderation is a context-sensitive form of agency. These findings contribute to debates on civil society in authoritarian and hybrid regimes, offering a framework for understanding why activism often unfolds outside the spotlight.
