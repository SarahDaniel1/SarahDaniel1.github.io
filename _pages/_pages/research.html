Working Papers

"The Neighborhoods in Our Minds: Subjective Geography and Civic Engagement"

Abstract

What drives individuals in cities to engage politically in their neighborhoods? While existing research emphasizes the objective social and physical environments in which individuals live, I argue that engagement is shaped first by how residents interpret and experience these environments. The size residents perceive their neighborhoods to be directly influences their civic engagement. I examine this possibility using evidence gathered through fieldwork in Nairobi, Kenya, including qualitative interviews, a face-to-face household survey, 1,169 mental maps containing residents’ perceived neighborhoods, and an embedded experiment manipulating perceived neighborhood size. I find that residents who perceive smaller neighborhoods are more likely to engage in civic initiatives and positive reciprocal exchanges. Observational mediation analysis shows that this relationship operates through increased perceptions of visibility and personal responsibility. This research offers additional insights into how residents of rapidly urbanizing cities across the Global South create alternative systems of support amid limited service provision.

"Protest or Partnership? How Nigerian Women’s Organizations Navigate Repression"
(with Mango Jane Angar, Leonardo R. Arriola, Matthew K. Gichohi, Liv Tønnessen, and Melanie Phillips)

Abstract

Despite Nigeria’s formal protections for civic activism, women’s rights organizations rarely engage in public protest. This article examines why protest remains an uncommon tactic among these groups even in moments of democratic openness and pressing gender policy debates. Drawing on interviews with women’s rights advocates and civil society leaders, we show that protest avoidance reflects a strategic adaptation to Nigeria’s hybrid political context. Organizations confront overlapping material constraints, reputational risks, and gendered norms that render confrontation costly and often counterproductive. In response, they adopt a repertoire of quiet advocacy by framing their work in technical terms, cultivating relationships with state actors, and engaging behind the scenes to influence policy. Rather than viewing the limited visibility of women’s movements as evidence of disengagement, we argue that tactical moderation is a context-sensitive form of agency. These findings contribute to debates on civil society in authoritarian and hybrid regimes, offering a framework for understanding why activism often unfolds outside the spotlight.
